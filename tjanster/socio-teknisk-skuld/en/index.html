Register of Socio-Technical Debt

Socio-technical debt (STeD) occurs when past social and/or technical decisions lead to inefficiencies or increased risks in designing, building, maintaining, managing or using digital solutions, which in turn hampers the organisation's ability to create value and reach its goals. The register collects every category of debt identified in my doctoral thesis and in literature published since, sorted by whether the debt resides in the technical artefact or in the social structures around it.

Social debt (social skuld)

27 categories

Social debt emerges from failures in communication, management and the organisation of development processes. Examples are poor team-management practices, flawed prioritisation and communication breakdown between business units and the IT department. The debt is accumulated through social decisions and resides in structures, roles and relationships rather than in the technology.

  • AI ethics debt (AI-etikskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TD
    • AI

    Deficiencies around ethical aspects of AI-based systems, such as algorithmic fairness, prediction bias, or a lack of transparency and accountability. Classified as social debt in the thesis because its roots lie in decisions and values rather than in the artefact.

    Sub-categories: Software fairness debt. Explainability debt is closely related but is placed under Transparency debt.

    Source: Bogner et al., 2021

  • Cognitive debt (Kognitiv skuld)

    • Social debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Deferred human costs that accumulate when repeated reliance on AI weakens independent reasoning, verification, domain understanding, or sensemaking capability.

    Sub-category of Competence debt. A more specific form of competence debt, tied to reliance on AI.

    Source: Ramamoorti et al., 2026; Fırat, 2026

  • Competence debt (Kompetensskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TyD
    • Sub-category

    Since the installed base is often built on elements of antiquated technology, conditions create a competence base optimised for maintenance and operations rather than for modernisation and the development of new technology.

    Sub-category of People debt. Sub-categories: Cognitive debt. Skill gaps are a form of people debt. Cognitive debt is in turn a more specific form of competence debt.

    Source: Magnusson et al., 2018

  • Compliance debt (Regelefterlevnadsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New

    Accumulated obligations created when regulatory, policy, assurance, or audit requirements are deferred and must be retrofitted later.

    Sub-categories: Privacy debt. Especially relevant for the public sector and AI. Privacy debt is placed here although it sits on the technical side.

    Source: Butt et al., 2025; Kapiyasheva et al., 2026

  • Consensus debt (Konsensusskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New

    Accumulated liabilities arising when important strategic or development decisions proceed without sufficient involvement of relevant stakeholders or without establishing adequate shared understanding and agreement. The resulting debt may later manifest as misalignment, renewed negotiation, resistance, rework, or difficulties in implementing and adapting decisions.

    A category in its own right. An explicit concept in Norling's thesis on consensus-based strategising in the public sector.

    Source: Norling, 2024; Lavallée et al., 2017

  • Cultural debt (Kulturskuld)

    • Social debt
    • NTD

    Arises from a technical decision that borrows against the organisation's culture. Such decisions can introduce team divisions, deteriorate communication, or even weaken leadership effectiveness. A culture that discourages collaboration, experimentation or learning from mistakes reinforces the debt.

    Source: Saeeda et al., 2023

  • Enterprise architecture debt (Verksamhetsarkitekturskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TD

    A metric that depicts the deviation of the currently present state of an enterprise from a hypothetical ideal state, viewed across the organisation's whole architecture rather than a single system.

    Source: Hacks et al., 2019

  • Governance debt (Styrningsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TyD

    Bias in the configuration of governance creates a governance optimised for stability that does not cater to the needs for innovation. Misaligned or excessively rigid IT governance structures.

    Sub-categories: Shadow-IT debt. Institutional debt is closely related but operates at the system level rather than the organisational level. Project management debt partly overlaps.

    Source: Magnusson et al., 2018

  • Information debt (Informationsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • DD

    Related to platform maintenance and evolvability, representing performance risks in an organisation's work processes. The accumulation of outdated, inconsistent or poorly structured information makes digital services harder to scale, integrate and adapt.

    Sub-categories: Semantic debt. Knowledge debt is kept apart by distinguishing stored information from organisational knowledge.

    Source: Rolland et al., 2018

  • Institutional debt (Institutionell skuld)

    • Social debt
    • New

    Accumulated systemic constraints created by outdated institutional arrangements, fragmented responsibilities, standards, rules, and governance structures.

    A category in its own right, emerging but especially relevant in a public-sector context. Closely related to Governance debt and Organisational debt but operates at the system level rather than within one organisation.

    Source: Ohno & Hashimoto, 2026

  • Knowledge debt (Kunskapsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New

    Accumulated loss of tacit knowledge, rationale, context, and design intent that later has to be reconstructed at substantial cost.

    A category in its own right, still emerging. Distinguished from Information debt by concerning organisational knowledge rather than stored information; also closely related to Documentation debt.

    Source: Tsai, 2026; Admile et al., 2025

  • Management debt (Ledningsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New

    Accumulated liabilities caused by short-term or inadequate managerial decisions concerning people, resources, technology adoption, and project execution.

    Sub-categories: Resource management debt, Project management debt. Umbrella concept for debts arising from managerial decisions.

    Source: Dayan-Akman et al., 2025

  • Organisational debt (Organisationsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • NTD

    The accumulating catalogue of changes that leaders should have made but didn't. Structural issues such as unclear hierarchies, siloed departments or misaligned responsibilities.

    Institutional debt and Management debt are closely related: management debt concerns decisions, organisational debt structures, and institutional debt rules and responsibilities at the system level.

    Source: Saeeda et al., 2023; Ahmad & Al-Baik, 2024

  • People debt (Personalskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TD
    • NTD

    People issues in the organisation that delay or hinder development activities, for example too few people having the necessary expertise due to delayed training or hiring, or over-reliance on a few key individuals.

    Sub-categories: Competence debt, Work-environment debt. Groups debts that concern access to the right competence.

    Source: Rios et al., 2018; Ahmad & Gustavsson, 2022

  • Process debt (Processkuld)

    • Social debt
    • TD
    • NTD

    Inefficient processes, where what the process was designed to handle is no longer appropriate. A suboptimal action or event with short-term advantages but long-term detrimental effects, such as outdated project-management methods, unnecessary bureaucracy or unsuitable procurement processes.

    Project management debt overlaps process debt and governance debt but is placed under Management debt.

    Source: Rios et al., 2018; Martini et al., 2020

  • Project management debt (Projektledningsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Accumulated future cost caused by deficiencies in planning, estimation, oversight, prioritisation, and project control.

    Sub-category of Management debt. Sub-categories: Project monitoring debt. Partly overlaps Process debt and Governance debt.

    Source: Kapiyasheva et al., 2026

  • Project monitoring debt (Projektuppföljningsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Debt caused by insufficient monitoring of project progress, tasks, risks, and dependencies, allowing problems to accumulate unnoticed.

    Sub-category of Project management debt.

    Source: Dayan-Akman et al., 2025

  • Reputation debt (Anseendeskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TyD

    Deficiencies in delivery capability over long periods of time create a reputation where users have a low level of trust for the IT department. Trust between the IT function and the business erodes.

    Source: Magnusson et al., 2018

  • Requirements debt (Kravskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TD
    • DD

    Trade-offs made with respect to what requirements the development team needs to implement or how to implement them. The distance between the optimal requirements specification and the actual system implementation.

    Sub-categories: Domain debt.

    Source: Rios et al., 2018; Li et al., 2015

  • Resource management debt (Resurshanteringsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Debt resulting from inadequate allocation, development, or monitoring of human, financial, and technical resources needed over the system lifecycle.

    Sub-category of Management debt.

    Source: Kapiyasheva et al., 2026

  • Shadow-IT debt (Skugg-IT-skuld)

    • Social debt
    • TyD
    • Sub-category

    Decentralised investment and non-sanctioned user-driven innovation, in parallel with official IT governance, creates a lack of synergies, efficiency and security.

    Sub-category of Governance debt. The debt is defined by technology being adopted outside sanctioned governance.

    Source: Magnusson et al., 2018

  • Software fairness debt (Rättviseskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Accumulated fairness deficiencies and unresolved bias that create future remediation costs and harmful or discriminatory outcomes.

    Sub-category of AI ethics debt.

    Source: de Souza Santos et al., 2025

  • Team collaboration debt (Teamsamarbetsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Debt arising when insufficient collaboration and coordination between roles or teams create rework, hand-off problems, and duplicated effort.

    Sub-category of Team debt.

    Source: Dayan-Akman et al., 2025

  • Team communication debt (Teamkommunikationsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Accumulated cost caused by communication breakdowns, weak information exchange, and insufficient shared understanding within or across teams.

    Sub-category of Team debt.

    Source: Kapiyasheva et al., 2026

  • Team debt (Teamskuld)

    • Social debt
    • New

    Accumulated social and structural frictions within a team that reduce collective effectiveness, coordination, and delivery capability.

    Sub-categories: Team collaboration debt, Team communication debt. Groups frictions within and between teams.

    Source: Kapiyasheva et al., 2026

  • User-satisfaction debt (Användarnöjdhetsskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TyD

    Deficiencies in user satisfaction affect users' willingness to work actively with the IT function to create improvements. Frustration with IT systems and low engagement.

    Source: Magnusson et al., 2018

  • Work-environment debt (Arbetsmiljöskuld)

    • Social debt
    • TyD
    • Sub-category

    Shortcomings in the work environment for IT workers create difficulty in attracting and retaining the competences needed for future needs.

    Sub-category of People debt.

    Source: Magnusson et al., 2018

Technical debt (teknisk skuld)

40 categories

Technical debt is a collection of design or implementation constructs that are expedient in the short term but set up a technical context that can make future changes more costly or impossible. It presents an actual or contingent liability whose impact is limited to internal system qualities, primarily maintainability and evolvability. It resides in artefacts such as code, architecture, databases and testing frameworks.

  • Accessibility debt (Tillgänglighetsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • New

    Accumulated accessibility barriers created when inclusive-design requirements are postponed and later require costly redesign or remediation.

    A category in its own right, conceptually distinct from Usability debt.

    Source: Indika et al., 2026; Lazar et al., 2026

  • Agentic technical debt (Agentisk teknisk skuld)

    • Technical debt
    • New

    System-level debt specific to agentic AI, arising from autonomous reasoning, persistent memory, tool orchestration, multi-agent coordination, and evolving agent behaviour.

    A standalone category for now. The literature describes it as an emerging umbrella concept that could in time group AI configuration debt, AI data debt and AI model debt, but that arrangement is not made yet.

    Source: Tukur et al., 2026

  • AI configuration debt (AI-konfigurationsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • AI
    • Sub-category

    Deficiencies around the configuration mechanisms of AI-based systems.

    Sub-category of Configuration debt.

    Source: Bogner et al., 2021

  • AI data debt (AI-dataskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • AI

    Deficiencies related to the collection, management and usage of data, both for training models and in production.

    Source: Bogner et al., 2021

  • AI model debt (AI-modellskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • AI

    Suboptimal practices in the design, training and management of AI models.

    Source: Bogner et al., 2021

  • Algorithm debt (Algoritmskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • Sub-category

    Sub-optimal implementations of algorithm logic.

    Sub-category of Code debt (implementation debt). Concerns sub-optimal implementation of algorithm logic.

    Source: Liu et al., 2020

  • Architectural debt (Arkitekturskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD

    Problems encountered in product architecture that affect architectural requirements. It has a significant influence on system success and, left unchecked, can cause expensive repercussions. Upgrades to systems with high architectural debt require three times the effort of well-structured architectures, and early architectural shortcuts increase brittleness and later rework costs.

    Source: Rios et al., 2018; Besker et al., 2016; MacCormack et al., 2012

  • Automation (build) debt (Automatiseringsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD

    In today's fast-paced, release-oriented development, almost any manual step in building a software system indicates automation debt. It is both a source of and a resolution approach to technical debt (also called build or DevOps debt).

    Sub-categories: Build debt, Pipeline debt. Groups debts in build, delivery and deployment flows.

    Source: Rosser & Norton, 2021

  • Automation test debt (Testautomatiseringsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • Sub-category

    The work involved in automating tests of previously developed functionality to support continuous integration and faster development cycles, plus the accumulation of issues such as poorly maintained test scripts, outdated test cases and lack of proper test coverage.

    Sub-category of Test debt (testing debt).

    Source: Rios et al., 2018; Wiklund et al., 2012

  • Build debt (Byggskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • Sub-category

    Issues that make the build task harder and unnecessarily time-consuming, for instance building and running unnecessary code and tests.

    Sub-category of Automation (build) debt. Overlaps automation debt to a high degree.

    Source: Rios et al., 2018; Morgenthaler et al., 2012

  • Code debt (implementation debt) (Kodskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • DD

    Problems found in the source code, that is poorly written code that violates best coding practices or coding rules, which reduces legibility and makes the code harder to maintain. This is Cunningham's original 1992 metaphor: ‘shipping first-time code is like going into debt’.

    Sub-categories: Algorithm debt.

    Source: Cunningham, 1992; Rios et al., 2018

  • Compatibility debt (Kompatibilitetsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • Sub-category

    A project's immature dependencies on other projects that cannot supply all qualified services, so that the current implementation is a temporary workaround.

    Sub-category of Dependency debt. Defined by immature dependencies on other projects.

    Source: Liu et al., 2020

  • Configuration debt (Konfigurationsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD

    Incurred when configuration mechanisms are not controlled with the same rigour as the software, when differences between configurations cannot easily be determined, or when redundant or unused configuration data exists.

    Sub-categories: AI configuration debt.

    Source: Rosser & Norton, 2021

  • Database debt (Databasskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD

    Accumulated through issues with data requirements, data architecture, database design, implementation and documentation, and through the typical dependence on a third party for the database package (commercial or open source).

    Sub-categories: Database-design debt.

    Source: Rosser & Norton, 2021

  • Database-design debt (Databasdesignskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • Sub-category

    Immature or suboptimal database design decisions that lag behind the optimal or desirable ones.

    Sub-category of Database debt. Database debt already covers database design.

    Source: Al-Barak & Bahsoon, 2016

  • Defect debt (Defektskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • Sub-category

    The trade-off between the short-term benefit of postponing bug-fixing and the long-term consequences of delaying it. Known defects that the development team agrees should be fixed but that are deferred due to competing priorities and limited resources.

    Sub-category of Quality debt. Quality debt concerns the total effort to fix defects; defect debt the known defects that have been deferred.

    Source: Akbarinasaji et al., 2016; Rios et al., 2018

  • Dependency debt (Beroendeskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • New

    Accumulated liabilities caused by external libraries, services, models, components, or data dependencies that become difficult, risky, or costly to change.

    Sub-categories: Compatibility debt. A category in its own right that fills a clear gap in figure 2 of the thesis.

    Source: Sculley et al., 2015; Arpteg et al., 2018

  • Deployment debt (Driftsättningsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • DD

    All the shortcuts, errors or mistakes that happen when deploying, for example rushed or incomplete deployment processes. More recent, mainly AI-related literature describes it as the accumulated engineering work created when prototypes or locally successful systems are repeatedly advanced without resolving the requirements for reliable production deployment and operation.

    Pipeline debt is closely related but concerns the pipeline itself rather than the transition to production.

    Source: Ernst et al., 2021; Asatiani & Torell, 2023; Costa, 2026

  • Depreciation debt (Föråldringsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD

    Software systems that have longevity incur depreciation debt because the rate of technology change often overtakes even a well-written software system.

    Source: Rosser & Norton, 2021

  • Design debt (Designskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • DD

    Tough time-to-market constraints and unanticipated integration or evolution issues lead to design trade-offs that cause flaws in the structure of a software system, such as violations of good object-oriented design principles or design shortcuts in customisation software. Design issues accumulate over time, making later modifications up to three times as costly as addressing them early.

    Source: Marinescu, 2012; Rios et al., 2018; Rolland et al., 2018; Zazworka et al., 2014

  • Documentation debt (Dokumentationsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • DD
    • NTD

    Missing, inconsistent, outdated or incomplete documentation, including outdated design specifications and lack of code comments. The only category recognised under all three umbrella concepts: technical, digital and non-technical debt.

    Source: Rios et al., 2018; Ernst et al., 2021; Ahmad et al., 2024

  • Domain debt (Domänskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • Sub-category

    The mis-representation of the application domain by an actual system.

    Sub-category of Requirements debt (social debt). Mis-representation of the domain usually originates in requirements work. Placed under requirements debt although it sits on the technical side.

    Source: Störrle & Ciolkowski, 2019

  • Energy debt (Energiskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Accumulated energy inefficiency in software that increases the future effort required to bring energy consumption back to an acceptable level.

    Sub-category of Sustainability debt.

    Source: Couto et al., 2020

  • Explainability debt (Förklarbarhetsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    The accumulated gap between the explainability, auditability, and stakeholder understanding required from an AI system and what the deployed system actually provides.

    Sub-category of Transparency debt. The AI-specific form of transparency debt. Also closely related to AI ethics debt.

    Source: Gandikota, Jegathesan et al., 2026

  • Infrastructure debt (Infrastrukturskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD
    • TyD

    Infrastructure issues that delay or hinder development activities, for example postponed upgrades. Deficiencies in infrastructure lead to potentially long lead times for future changes and require modernisation and new investment before new innovation with concrete benefits can be delivered.

    Source: Rios et al., 2018; Alves et al., 2014; Magnusson et al., 2018

  • Integration debt (Integrationsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD

    The effort involved in connecting systems or components together to achieve desired functionality. Non-standard connections, proprietary interfaces and niche standards increase the debt.

    Sub-categories: Service debt.

  • Modelling and simulation debt (Modellerings- och simuleringsskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • TD

    As model-based engineering becomes standard practice, the models become part of the technical baseline and are themselves subject to technical debt.

    Source: Rosser & Norton, 2021

  • Performance debt (Prestandaskuld)

    • Technical debt
    • New

    Accumulated future cost caused by deferred performance, scalability, latency, throughput, or resource-efficiency improvements.

    A category in its own right, with a smaller research base than code and architectural debt.

    • Technical debt
    • New
    • Sub-category

    Debt embedded in brittle, tightly coupled, poorly reproducible, or weakly governed build, data, ML, or deployment pipelines.

    Sub-category of Automation (build) debt. Especially relevant for AI/ML systems.

    The quantity of effort required for identifying, governing, controlling, communicating and protecting the software system with regard to data protection and regulatory compliance throughout the development lifecycle.

How the debts interrelate

The division into two domains gives analytical clarity, but in practice the debts reinforce each other. Weak governance and siloed decision-making produce inconsistent architectures and fragmented codebases, and outdated systems frustrate users, create resistance to change and erode trust between IT and the business. Social debt is often the fuel for technical debt, and persistent technical debt in turn breeds new social debt.

Example: social debt drives technical debt

Organisational debt can directly trigger the accumulation of technical debt, as weak governance or siloed decision-making leads to inconsistent architectures or fragmented codebases.

Cultural debt and organisational debt often shape inefficient development processes, which in turn generate technical debt in the artefacts themselves.

Example: technical debt drives social debt

Persistent technical debt in outdated systems frustrates users, creates resistance to change and weakens trust between the IT function and the business, that is user-satisfaction and reputation debt.

The thesis's municipal studies show that the debt is above all social: fragmented governance, procurement dependencies and organisational inertia drive it more than deficiencies in the code.

History of the concept: from technical to socio-technical debt

The debt metaphor was born in the world of programming but has over three decades grown into a way of describing the whole socio-technical system in which software is developed, maintained and used. The timeline summarises the development described in the thesis's introductory chapters (section 2.2), which culminates in the unifying concept of socio-technical debt.

  1. 1992

    The metaphor is coined

    Ward Cunningham describes ‘quick-and-dirty’ programming as a loan: a little debt speeds up development, provided it is paid back promptly with a rewrite.

    ‘Shipping first-time code is like going into debt. A little debt speeds development so long as it is paid back promptly with a rewrite. […] Every minute spent on not-quite-right code counts as interest on that debt.’

    The metaphor gains power because it also helps non-technical decision-makers in finance and management understand the risks of poor practice. Lutz (1993) shows that the debt can also be strategically viable, like borrowing capital in the business world.

  2. 2003 and 2009

    Interest, principal and Fowler's quadrant

    Martin Fowler simplifies the metaphor to the choice between a ‘messy but quick’ and a ‘clean but time-consuming’ solution. Just like a loan, the debt demands interest payments in the form of extra effort in all future development, until the principal is paid down by refactoring. Fowler later distinguishes deliberate from inadvertent, and prudent from reckless, debt. The concept thereby becomes a shared language for developers, architects and managers.

  3. 2012–2013

    Expansion and a warning against dilution

    Kruchten et al. (2012) and Tom et al. (2013) expand technical debt to the architectural and process levels, but also note that inconsistent terminology risks weakening the concept's analytical precision.

  4. 2014 and 2018

    Technology debt (TyD)

    Magnusson and Bygstad define technology debt as ‘the accumulated obligation owned by the current CIO (debtor) to a future CIO (creditor), where previous decisions limit prospective decisions’. The debt does not arise in the code but between the artefacts, in paths and dependencies: a proprietary enterprise system allows rapid deployment but locks the organisation into costly upgrade cycles. Three main groups are described (organisation, users, technology) with subtypes such as competence, work-environment, user-satisfaction, reputation, infrastructure, shadow-IT and governance debt (Magnusson et al., 2018).

  5. 2015

    Social debt

    Tamburri et al. define social debt as ‘a cumulative and increasing cost in the current state of things, connected to invisible and negative effects within a development community’. It is the first distinct formulation of the idea that debt can reside in collaboration and relationships rather than in code.

  6. 2016

    The research field agrees on a definition

    Leading debt researchers meet at a seminar to harmonise the concept. The outcome (Avgeriou et al., 2016) defines technical debt as design or implementation constructs that are expedient in the short term but set up a technical context that can make future changes more costly or impossible, with impact limited to internal system qualities. Three key elements are highlighted: the debt (the suboptimal constructs), the interest (future extra effort) and the principal (the cost of removing the debt). It is this definition that delimits the technical domain in the register above.

  7. 2018

    A taxonomy of 15 types, and digital debt (DD)

    Rios et al. compile, in a tertiary study, a taxonomy of 15 sub-types of technical debt, from requirements and testing to documentation, process and people, while warning that the concept is being used as a ‘catch-all’ for any obstacle in development. The taxonomy has since been substantially extended and forms the backbone of appendix 1 of the thesis.

    The same year, Rolland et al. define digital debt as ‘an organization's cumulative build-up of technical and informational obligations related to the maintenance and evolvability of its platform and infrastructure’. The concept shifts focus from individual technical flaws to the systemic outcome of postponed modernisation, and extends the metaphor to the information layer. Ernst et al. (2021) and Asatiani & Torell (2023) specify eight sub-categories, of which deployment, social and information debt are the most distinctive.

  8. 2021

    AI-specific debts

    Bogner et al. map technical debt in AI-based systems and describe four new types: AI configuration, AI data, AI model and AI ethics debt. The metaphor follows into a new generation of technology.

  9. 2023–2024

    Non-technical debt (NTD)

    Saeeda et al. define non-technical debt as the accumulation of suboptimal or immature decisions, compromises or workarounds in the non-technical aspects of development: poor communication, inefficient processes, misaligned teams, weak leadership. Five sub-categories are arranged (process, people, cultural, social and organisational debt), and Ahmad et al. (2024) add documentation debt. Non-technical debt often acts as an amplifier: when processes, structures or human factors fail, even well-built systems do not deliver value.

    The overlaps now become clear: process debt appears in both the TD and NTD literature, cultural debt aligns closely with socio-technical debt, and documentation debt is recognised under all three rubrics. The field needs a framework that orders the categories without diluting the metaphor.

  10. 2023

    Transparency debt in a municipal context

    The thesis's first study (Persson et al., 2023) identifies transparency debt: closed code makes it hard to know what data is stored about citizens and which algorithms are used when cases are evaluated. The study of the building-permit systems of a Swedish municipality also shows that departments work in silos and that citizens have only a passive role as system users.

  11. 2024

    Socio-technical debt (STeD) as a unifying concept

    The debate on the metaphor's boundaries continues: one side holds that the expansion is necessary to capture the socio-technical reality (Ahmad & Al-Baik, 2024; Avgeriou et al., 2025), the other that the concept is groaning under ever more categories (Tom et al., 2013; Besker et al., 2016). Riquet et al. (2024) propose socio-technical debt as a unifying construct: organisations cannot meaningfully separate technical debt in artefacts from the organisational and social dynamics that shape development.

    ‘A phenomenon that occurs when past social and/or technical decisions lead to inefficiencies or increased risks in designing, building, maintaining, managing, or using software engineering artifacts. This, in turn, hampers an organization's ability to create value, achieve strategic goals, or pursue the best course of action.’

    The debt has three defining characteristics: it is cumulative (the longer it remains, the higher the cost), it is costly to eliminate, and it is inevitable in practice since trade-offs and compromises are part of all development work.

  12. 2025

    The thesis: the categories are sorted into two domains

    Using Riquet's framework as a lens, the thesis arranges all previously articulated categories into two overarching domains: artefact-related debt (technical debt in code, architecture, databases and testing frameworks) and social-related debt (debt accumulated through social decisions about communication, governance and resource allocation). Categories previously counted as technical, such as requirements, process and AI ethics debt, are placed on the social side because their roots lie in social contexts. That is the classification reproduced in the register on this page.

    The framework is then used as a diagnostic tool in five studies and 25 complementary expert interviews in the Swedish municipal sector. Nine problems driving the accumulation of debt are identified and mapped to design principles and architecture blueprints. The conclusion: in the municipalities, the debt is above all social. The classification is not complete, since the metaphor keeps evolving as new contexts and technologies emerge.

  13. 2026

    The register is extended with post-thesis categories

    A review of literature published after the thesis, and of a few older categories not included in figure 2, adds further categories to the register tagged ‘New’. On the social side these include consensus, team, management, compliance, knowledge and institutional debt; on the technical side dependency, pipeline, performance, accessibility, sustainability, explainability and semantic debt, as well as agentic technical debt. Several of them are arranged as sub-categories of others, which is stated on the respective card. At the same time, some of the thesis's original categories are arranged in the same way, for example database-design debt under database debt and shadow-IT debt under governance debt.

The four umbrella concepts

Before socio-technical debt was introduced, research had created several parallel umbrella concepts to distinguish between different layers and sources of debt. They do not replace technical debt in its traditional sense but complement it at different levels of abstraction, from code quality to organisational strategy. The register states for each category which of the concepts it was defined within.

  • TD

    Technical debt

    Debt in the software itself: code, design, architecture, testing, documentation. The harmonised definition (Avgeriou et al., 2016) limits the impact to the system's maintainability and evolvability. Rios et al. (2018) provide the taxonomy.

    Cunningham, 1992; Avgeriou et al., 2016; Rios et al., 2018

  • DD

    Digital debt

    An organisation's cumulative build-up of technical and informational obligations related to the maintenance and evolvability of its platform and infrastructure. An impediment to digital transformation that also covers the information layer.

    Rolland et al., 2018; Ernst et al., 2021; Asatiani & Torell, 2023

  • TyD

    Technology debt

    Obligations arising from strategic, infrastructural, organisational and governance choices, often at executive level: procurement strategies, vendor dependencies and governance models that bind future decisions.

    Magnusson & Bygstad, 2014; Magnusson et al., 2018

  • NTD

    Non-technical debt

    Suboptimal decisions, compromises and workarounds in the non-technical aspects of development: processes, people, culture, social relations and organisational structure. Often an amplifier of technical debt.

    Saeeda et al., 2023; Ahmad et al., 2024
